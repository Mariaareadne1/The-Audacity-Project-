Our story

Thirteen years at an all-girls school. Zero mentions of venture capital.

Maria spent her entire K–12 education at The Bryn Mawr School in Baltimore — an all-girls school she loved, with strong academics and serious science classes. And in thirteen years there, nobody ever said the words "venture capital." Not once. Startups weren't a thing people talked about. The career menu, as far as anyone knew, was: doctor, lawyer, maybe engineer if you were the math type.

The closest thing to an exception was a short after-school finance program — a few weeks of investing basics, one evening at a time, capped by a trip to New York to meet alumnae working in finance. It was small. It was also, looking back, the single most important preview of a professional world that Maria got in high school. It just stopped at finance. Nobody extended the line to founders, to technology, to the people who fund new companies.

So the discovery happened by accident, in college. Maria got to Barnard, started studying computer science, and applied — almost didn't, actually — to Columbia's biggest entrepreneurship club. She got in, and a door opened onto an entire world: founders, funds, demo days, term sheets, people in their twenties building real companies. Her first thought was how exciting it all was. Her second thought was harder to shake: why am I only hearing about this now?

The men around her weren't only hearing about it now. Many of them had known about this world since middle school — from parents, older brothers, group chats, YouTube. They walked into college already fluent. That fluency compounds: it's who applies to the club, who feels entitled to cold email a founder, who raises their hand for the internship, and eventually, who raises money. The gap was never talent. It was a head start.

Men have the audacity to go for things they're not qualified for. Women talk themselves out of applying. That difference is trainable — in both directions.

Maria's mom has a saying that became the seed of this whole organization: men have an audacity that women don't — the absurd courage to go for things they're not qualified for. The research backs her up. Women consistently apply to fewer opportunities than equally qualified men, hold themselves to a stricter standard of "ready," and are dramatically underrepresented among funded founders. In recent Y Combinator batches, fewer than a quarter of startups had even one woman on the founding team.

You can read that as a discouraging statistic. We read it as a curriculum.

What we're doing about it

The Audacity Project teaches high school girls two things at once. The first is knowledge: what startups actually are, how venture capital works, what equity means, how fundraising happens — the vocabulary that some kids get at the dinner table and most girls never get at all. The second is the thing the name promises: audacity itself, practiced like a skill. Every session assigns a dare. Cold email a founder. Connect with a woman at a VC firm. Pitch your idea to someone who will push back. Small, real acts of boldness, repeated until they stop feeling like acts.

We started planning in fall 2025 and launched in January 2026 at Bryn Mawr — deliberately tiny, under ten girls, run as close mentorship rather than a lecture series. We wanted to learn what worked before we scaled anything. By the end of the semester, more than fifty students had asked about joining.

That told us what we needed to know.

Where this goes next

This fall, the program becomes the real thing: a full eight-session curriculum at Bryn Mawr covering startups, venture capital, fundraising, and building — ending in a pitch day judged by women who actually do this work. From there: more all-girls schools in Baltimore, investors and sponsors behind the program, and the capstone we're fundraising toward — an all-expenses-paid New York trip where girls walk into real VC firms and female-founded startups and meet women who were once exactly where they're sitting.

Because the whole point is this: none of it should happen by accident. Not the way it did for us.
